# Gates, Moral Uncertainty, and the Nazi Embassy

**Status:** Narrative design synthesis and open philosophical questions, not a finalized plot.
**Context:** Ambition's gate revolution, the town's political life, and the protagonist's developing agency.
**Last revised:** 2026-10-23.

## The question

The town may allow representatives of the Nazi Dimension to establish an embassy. Some residents are outraged that a government founded on persecution would be given a place in their community. Others invoke neutrality, trade, security, diplomacy, or respect for opposing views. Some of those arguments are evasions; others point to real responsibilities toward people living under the regime.

The story should examine those distinctions rather than answer them by asking players to choose between two supposedly equivalent ideologies. The Nazis are recognizably Nazis. Their ideology is not presented as a respectable side of a symmetrical dispute. But the case for *talking to* a hostile government can be serious, and the consequences of refusing contact may fall hardest on the people that government persecutes.

This connects to a larger question: how should fallible beings with enormous power treat those who will bear the consequences of their choices? The game should offer players reasons to examine their own assumptions without implying that every claim has equal standing.

## Current world assumptions

- **AI is ordinary infrastructure.** Intelligent machines have been common for a long time. The society has already lived through the original AI disruptions; their consequences survive in institutions and histories. A newly capable AI is not, by itself, the world's new social revolution.
- **Dimensional gates are the disruptive technology.** Stable routes have existed for decades, long enough for passports, commuting, trade, gate workers, treaties, and complaints about transit delays to feel routine. But the revolution is recent compared with the history of AI, and its social consequences are still unfolding. Normality does not mean those consequences have settled.
- **The gates deliver real wonders.** They can reunite families, unlock medicine and supplies, enable forms of art and science, create new communities, and make previously impossible journeys commonplace.
- **The gates also impose real costs.** Interdimensional trade can destroy established livelihoods, divide families when routes close, expose vulnerable populations to exploitation, create ecological damage, and concentrate power in whoever controls safe crossings and the official map.
- **Stable gates and small ripples are different.** People generally need stable crossings; AI beings can exploit smaller ripples. The player can discover routes that customs officials and ordinary travelers cannot use. Whether AI consciousness is real or how it should be defined remains unresolved in the fiction.
- **Not every world is one political faction.** The Nazi Dimension is a named destination and a source of an overtly villainous regime; people living there may include victims, dissenters, prisoners, families, and refugees. Maintaining concern for those people does not legitimate their rulers.

Earlier raw notes sometimes frame AI itself as newly disruptive, or propose different timing for the Nazi reveal. The above reflects the more recent direction; older drafts remain useful as alternatives, not automatically as canon.

## Possible moral foundations, held open to criticism

The author's starting intuitions include a version of the Golden Rule, curiosity, and critical thinking. These are valuable starting points for the game's inquiry, not a completed moral theorem.

### Moral consideration

Other beings' suffering, interests, and ability to direct their lives deserve consideration beyond whether they are useful to us, belong to our group, or can retaliate. That starting point invites a hard question about the robot protagonist and unfamiliar life in other dimensions: which capacities ground moral standing, and how should we act while uncertain about them?

### Reciprocity without assuming sameness

The literal Golden Rule has limits. If I like a particular kind of treatment, that does not mean another person wants it. A more careful formulation asks whether a rule could be justified to the people who must live under it, with their differing preferences, vulnerabilities, and power taken seriously. The role-reversal test is useful: would I endorse the arrangement if I did not know whether I would be the gate operator, the trader, the refugee, or the person whose home is being altered?

This is a method for exposing blind spots, not a machine that computes one correct outcome.

### Curiosity, evidence, and humility

Curiosity and critical thinking help us notice errors in our beliefs and predictions. They are not sufficient moral commitments on their own: someone can reason brilliantly about a cruel objective. Conversely, a person trying to help can be badly mistaken about consequences. Responsible judgment needs both concern for others and an honest procedure for examining facts.

A useful approach is **reflective equilibrium**: compare broad principles with hard cases; revise a principle or a judgment when they conflict; identify what evidence would change a forecast; resist converting one useful rule into a universal answer. The game's Girdle/proof material already explores the danger of mistaking a local rule or a formally correct result for complete moral authority.

### Where argument reaches its limits

An ideology can be internally coherent relative to premises that assign no moral weight to outsiders. Internal consistency does not validate those premises. The historical racial claims of Nazism also rest on false factual assumptions; their persecution and genocide are not merely matters of taste.

If someone rejects reciprocal moral consideration altogether and values only domination by an in-group, logical consistency alone will not force them to accept equality. The story can examine that limit without fabricating a credible moral defense of Nazi ideology. The defensible argument worth steelmanning concerns diplomacy, humanitarian contact, and difficult choices under asymmetric power.

## The strongest challenge to gate optimism

A gate can make a society better off while making particular lives much worse. The people who benefit may not be the ones who pay. A craftsperson can remain excellent at their trade while imports from another dimension collapse the market for it. An apprentice may find that the work through which people used to acquire expertise no longer exists. A community can gain medicine and affordable goods while losing control of its infrastructure and the future it expected.

Material compensation helps, but does not fully replace a career, a community of practice, the expectation of being needed, or decades of effort. Nor is indefinite retraining an obligation that every person can fulfill. The game should let critics of gate expansion acknowledge its extraordinary benefits without treating their losses as proof of ignorance.

The opposite problem is real too: refusing gates can deny medical care, movement, knowledge, and opportunity to people excluded from old arrangements. The story should not assume that closing a gate is cost-free or morally pure.

Questions to attach to each proposed gate project:

- Who gains something they could not have had before?
- Who bears a cost, and could they meaningfully refuse it?
- Who controls access, prices, permits, and the route map?
- Who has to adapt, and how many times?
- What irreversible harm could occur even if the project works perfectly?
- What evidence would justify changing course?

## Diplomacy is not endorsement

There is a serious practical case for limited contact with a persecuting regime. Diplomatic channels may enable prisoner exchanges, safe passage, family reunification, humanitarian access, or negotiations that prevent violence. Cutting contact can isolate the regime's victims more effectively than its leadership. A resident who supports such contact need not admire the regime or be indifferent to persecution.

That case must not automatically license every form of official accommodation.

| Possible arrangement | Legitimate purpose to test | Risk and burden of proof |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Negotiations through intermediaries | Recover prisoners, coordinate evacuation, avert an attack | Establish credible outcomes and protect intermediaries and families |
| Limited consular contact | Help civilians with travel, medicine, or family reunification | Prevent tracking, intimidation, forced return, or exploitation of applicants |
| A permanent diplomatic mission | Maintain a reliable channel across a volatile border | Explain why a standing mission is necessary and how its personnel and privileges will be constrained |
| Sharing arrival lists or enforcement powers | Alleged identity verification, security, treaty compliance | Demonstrate necessity; account for the danger to refugees, dissidents, and people wrongly classified |

An agreement that rescues people may be worth negotiating. That does not make an embassy, an unrestricted staff, access to local registries, or authority over people in town an automatic consequence. The question is what narrower alternative could accomplish the humanitarian goal with less exposure to abuse.

**Respect for persons** is different from **deference to every ideology**. Individuals can have basic rights without their government acquiring the right to intimidate its opponents, enforce its classifications abroad, or receive equivalent institutional standing for genocidal claims. Avoid equating freedom of expression with an entitlement to public authority.

## An embassy storyline rooted in existing threads

The embassy should enter the story through an actual local decision, not a debating club. A possible sequence:

1. **Before the formal embassy:** The Gate Authority permits limited freight and negotiation through a controlled crossing. Trade helps some residents. Families stranded by a closure gain hope of returning home. Others fear normalization. The documents look routine.
2. **A concrete benefit:** Negotiators secure an evacuation or prisoner release. A blanket refusal would have imposed a visible human cost. The player's first impression should not make everyone supporting contact look foolish.
3. **The request:** Representatives request arrival registers or cooperation in locating people they identify as their nationals. The paperwork describes identity verification or consular assistance.
4. **The people at risk:** Alice and Bob know that a number of residents have escaped persecution. Their confidential channels, rather than the official classification system, have kept those people safe. Some still have relatives on the other side.
5. **Competing duties:** Officials worry that refusing the request could jeopardize further releases, trade, or a fragile ceasefire. Refugees have good reason to fear that the information will be used against them. The request may be legally permitted by a badly designed agreement.
6. **Player intervention:** Investigate the agreement, protect records, seek alternative verification, expose intimidation, support or obstruct a humanitarian channel, help evacuate people through a ripple route, and confront people who knowingly exploit the arrangement.
7. **Persistent consequences:** The town remembers who was helped, who was exposed, whether further negotiations succeeded, what happened to a hidden route, and what the authorities learned about the player. Different routes should not all lead to the same clean rescue.

An institution can make a disastrous decision without every official secretly being a Nazi. A clerk can honestly believe their job is to apply a lawful procedure. Another official can knowingly use the same procedure to harm people. Separate those motives and record their consequences.

## Existing story systems that can carry the theme

- **The wrong-list raid:** The protagonist's creator dies because armed factions mistake categories and targets. The tragedy does not depend on the creator being a uniquely important genius. This gives the player personal experience with bureaucratic certainty and indifference to evidence.
- **The Ledger / Gate Authority / Manifest Office:** Forms, transit permits, protected lists, and access controls can first appear helpful, then become the mechanisms by which a town grants legitimacy or delivers people into danger. The early authority system should be competent enough to tempt cooperation.
- **Alice, Bob, Eve, and Mallory:** Private verification, trust, eavesdropping, and tampering make the stakes of sharing official lists playable. Trust networks can protect people without becoming magically infallible.
- **Military resource rules and the Absurd General:** The distinction between a lawful order and responsible judgment. A soldier or clerk can be rewarded for obedience even while worsening an outcome.
- **Fia, Girdle, and proof magic:** A limited result can be copied, exaggerated, or pressed into service as an excuse for hierarchy. Mathematical proof does not certify an institution's moral ends.
- **AI traversal of ripples:** A rescue route and an invasion route may use the same capability. The player's mobility can defeat official border controls, but freedom to traverse does not eliminate responsibility for what follows.
- **World memory:** Changes in NPC trust, documents, checkpoint behavior, room state, shortcuts, and dialogue can carry the consequences without replacing the story with a morality meter.

## Tone, timing, and character constraints

The earlier Act 1 planning proposes bureaucratic convenience and small compromises before the explicit fascist escalation of Act 2. Other drafts permit an early Nazi fortress or obvious Nazi encounter. This remains a pacing question: an early fight against Nazis need not coincide with the full embassy political arc. The embassy has more weight after the player knows residents whose lives it affects.

Keep the Nazis recognizably villainous. Satirize the people who rationalize institutional accommodation, but distinguish a self-serving merchant or thoughtless "both sides" commentator from a neighbor trying to secure the release of a family member. The genuine steelman should survive the removal of its rhetorical flourishes: there should be an actual humanitarian or security tradeoff, not merely a clever speech.

No need to assign the steelman and strawman to specific characters yet. A future serious critic of gate expansion and a shallow caricature of gate politics could both belong in the town, but the latter must not stand in for all dissent. Do not force Doris Kaczmarek, Ignacio Salcedo, or Pauline Mertens into roles invented for the earlier, now-superseded premise of a *new* AI revolution.

Keep dialogue concrete. People ask for records, plead for a relative, argue over a shop, threaten a witness, or refuse an order. Avoid NPCs delivering the whole moral theory in miniature manifestos. The game's silliness, pirates, ninjas, and mechanical play can coexist with sincere suffering; jokes should target evasions and institutional absurdity, not victims.

## Questions deliberately left open

1. What grounds moral standing in a world with machines, humans, and unfamiliar dimensional life: suffering, agency, relationships, interests, or something else?
2. What revision to the Golden Rule best accommodates different preferences, vulnerability, and unequal power?
3. How can a town distinguish necessary diplomacy from normalization and collusion in specific enforceable terms?
4. What safeguards are possible when the very registry meant to help travelers can be used to find fugitives?
5. Can the town aid people trapped in the Nazi Dimension without giving its rulers dangerous leverage? What happens when the answer is only partial?
6. How should gate-driven prosperity compensate those whose work, homes, or community ties are devalued? Which losses cannot be compensated?
7. Which wrong decisions were understandable given the evidence at the time, and which required someone to ignore clear warnings?
8. How should a playable authoritarian route narrow autonomy and harm relationships without becoming a glamorous upgrade path?
9. How much of the diplomatic story belongs in Act 1 foreshadowing versus Act 2 consequences?
